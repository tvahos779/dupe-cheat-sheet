The 9mm Field Guide

Forget caliber wars and YouTube hype. Here's what actually separates the nine pistols above — written the way we'd explain it to a friend at the counter.

Striker vs. hammer-fired

Striker-fired (APX A1, both Glocks, Echelon, Hellcat Pro, Mako): one consistent trigger pull, no external hammer, simpler manual of arms. This is the modern default for a reason — nothing to decock, nothing to snag, same shot every time.

Hammer-fired DA/SA (M9, Px4 Storm): the first pull is long and heavy — think of it as a built-in safety for a gun that lives in a nightstand drawer — then every shot after is a light, crisp single-action. Fans swear the heavy first pull prevents more accidents than any mechanical safety.

Single-action (Micro 9): a tiny 1911. The best trigger feel here, full stop — but you carry it cocked with the thumb safety on, and you train that way or you don't carry it.

What actually matters

  • Ergonomics first. A gun that points where your hand naturally aims beats any spec sheet. Interchangeable backstraps (APX, Echelon) are worth real money.
  • Trigger quality = practical accuracy. A clean break and short reset matter more than the pull-weight number in the ad copy.
  • Get the optic cut. Even if you never mount a dot, optic-ready models are where the deals and the resale value live now. The irons-only guns are the ones getting cleared out.
  • Aftermarket support is a feature. Holsters, spare mags, sights: Glock has the deepest ecosystem, Springfield and the APX are close behind, Kimber is slimmer. Budget for at least three magazines — factory mags run $25–45 each.

Brand quick-takes

Beretta — Best ergonomics per dollar right now, because Beretta is subsidizing it with rebates. The APX's trigger beats a stock Glock's, and the M9/Px4 pair are the softest shooters on the page. If you're price-sensitive, start here.

Glock — Not the best trigger, not the best grip, and still the right answer for a lot of people: unmatched parts, holsters, and $25 magazines everywhere on Earth. Buy the V series without fear — it's the same gun, current production.

 

Springfield — The Hellcat Pro owns the carry niche outright, and the Echelon is the smartest duty-gun design of the bunch (chassis system, plate-free optics). Springfield is where the innovation is happening.

Kimber — Two different plays: the Micro 9 is a craftsmanship buy (metal, fit, that 1911 trigger), while the Mako is the value buy nobody talks about — Kimber internals at a street price under the Hellcat. Either way you're getting better finish work than the price suggests.

The 10-second version: Carry gun → Hellcat Pro or Mako. First pistol, tight budget → APX A1 at $299.99 net and don't look back. Nightstand → M9 or Px4. One gun for everything → Glock 45 V or Echelon.

Buying a Gun Online: FFL Transfer Basics

First-timers picture a pistol arriving in a mailbox. It doesn't work that way — here's the actual process, start to finish. It's easier than it sounds.

How it works

  1. Pick your local FFL dealer first — any gun shop or pawn shop with a Federal Firearms License. Call and confirm they accept online transfers and ask the fee (typically $25–50). Do this before you order.
  2. Order online. At checkout you select that dealer; the seller ships the firearm to the FFL — never to your house.
  3. The FFL logs it in and calls you when it arrives, usually 2–5 business days.
  4. You go in, do the paperwork: ATF Form 4473 plus a NICS background check, pay the transfer fee, and take your gun home.

Know before you buy

  • True cost = price + shipping + transfer fee. A $400 pistol can be $460 out the door. Run that math before deciding an online deal beats the local shelf.
  • Illinois buyers: you need a valid FOID card, and Illinois law adds a 72-hour waiting period on every firearm purchase — the clock starts at application, so the gun may sit at the dealer a few days. Normal, not a problem with your order.
  • Rebates are on you. Beretta's current promo ($100 back on the APX A1, M9, and Px4 above) is a mail-in/online claim — keep your receipt and photograph the serial number. Purchases by Nov 30, 2026, claims by Dec 31, 2026.
  • Inspect before you accept the transfer. Once the 4473 is done, the gun is yours — any defect becomes a warranty conversation with the manufacturer instead of a return.

Shotguns: Pump vs. Semi-Auto

The shortest honest version: a pump is a tool you operate, a semi-auto is a machine that operates. Both are right answers — for different jobs and budgets.

Pump vs. semi-auto

Pump: you are the gas system, so it cycles anything — light target loads, 3″ magnums, even low-recoil specialty shells that choke a semi. Cheapest path to a dead-reliable gun. The failure mode is you: short-stroke the action under stress and it jams. Train the full, violent rack.

Semi-auto: faster follow-up shots and noticeably softer recoil. Gas guns (Benelli M4's ARGO system) are the most forgiving with varied loads; inertia guns (Benelli M2) are lighter and cleaner-running but want full-power shells. Expect to pay two to four times a good pump's price.

Mossberg vs. Benelli

Mossberg — The 500 is the everyman pump: aluminum receiver keeps it light and cheap, controls are the best in the class (top tang safety, slide release behind the trigger guard), and parts grow on trees. The 590A1 is the same gun overbuilt to a military spec — heavier barrel, metal where the 500 uses polymer. It's the one you hand down.

Benelli — The Nova/SuperNova are polymer-over-steel pumps chambered for 3.5″ shells with the ComforTech stock chewing up recoil — the duck-blind and bad-weather choice, starting at $749 MSRP per Benelli USA. The M4 is the semi-auto benchmark the Marines carry; street prices hover near $1,900 and it's worth it only if you'll actually run it hard. No verifiable Benelli deal exists today — but Beretta's A300 Patrol (card above) is what happens when a retailer breaks a MAP lock, and it's the semi-auto we'd buy at $989.

Load note: for home defense, pattern #4 or 00 buckshot at your actual in-home distances — every shotgun patterns differently, and "birdshot is for birds" is the whole rule.

Rifles: Barrels & Actions, Quick and Honest

Two rifles are carded above; a hundred more exist. These are the two spec areas that actually decide whether a rifle suits you — action type and barrel — minus the forum mythology.

Actions: what cycles the next round

Bolt-action (Savage Axis, Tikka T3x, Bergara B-14 class): you run the bolt by hand. Nothing to go wrong, the most accurate action per dollar, and the default for hunting and precision. A smooth bolt and a good trigger matter more than the brand on the receiver.

Blowback semi-auto (the 10/22): the cartridge's own pressure pushes the bolt back. Simple, cheap, and perfect for .22 LR — it doesn't scale to centerfire rifle power, which is why rimfires use it and ARs don't.

Gas semi-auto / direct impingement (the M&P15): tapped gas cycles the action, so follow-up shots are as fast as your trigger finger. Mid-length gas systems (Sport III) run softer and easier on parts than carbine-length — the one spec to check on any budget AR.

Barrels: twist is the number that matters

  • Twist rate matches bullet weight. Rifling is written like 1:8″ — one full spin every 8 inches. A 1:8″ 5.56 barrel stabilizes 55–77 grain bullets; 1:7″ favors the heavy end, 1:9″ the light end. Buy the twist for the ammo you'll actually shoot.
  • Length buys velocity, not accuracy. A 16″ AR barrel is handier and gives up only ~100–150 fps to a 20″. In .22 LR, anything past ~16″ adds nothing — the powder is done burning.
  • Free-floated + threaded is the modern baseline. A handguard that doesn't touch the barrel keeps sling and bipod pressure off your groups; a threaded muzzle means a brake, comp, or suppressor later without a gunsmith bill.
  • Cold hammer-forged vs. button-rifled is a longevity argument, not an accuracy one at normal round counts. Either outlasts most shooters.

The 10-second version: Learn on the 10/22 — cheap ammo, no recoil, a lifetime of parts. One centerfire rifle for everything → the Sport III. Chasing groups or deer at distance → bolt gun, and spend the savings on the optic.

Accessories: What Actually Matters

The gun gets the glory, but these are the parts that decide whether your setup actually works when you pick it up. The same rules we card by.

Holsters: fit is retention

A holster is molded to one gun — and if it carries a light, to one gun-and-light combination. Change the light, change the holster. "Universal fit" means it fits nothing well.

Passive retention (friction plus a molded trigger-guard click) is right for concealed carry under a garment. Active retention — Safariland's ALS thumb lever on the 7378 above — physically locks the gun in until your thumb drives the release as part of the draw. That's the standard for open carry, duty use, and any gun that could get grabbed or snagged.

The tests: with the unloaded gun holstered, turn it upside down and shake — it shouldn't fall out. The draw shouldn't fight the lock. The trigger guard must be fully covered, and the mouth must stay open so you can reholster one-handed. Fail any of those and it's a pouch, not a holster.

Magazines: OEM vs. aftermarket

The magazine is the #1 cause of malfunctions in an otherwise good gun — it's a spring, a follower, and feed lips holding tight tolerances, and cheap copies get all three wrong. For a carry or defensive gun, buy factory OEM or Magpul and don't experiment.

"Aftermarket" splits in two. Magpul — and Mec-Gar, who quietly manufacture the "factory" mags for several big brands — are engineered and tested to OEM grade; the D-60 above is the proof. No-name bargain mags are range toys: fine for malfunction drills, never for the gun you'd grab at 3 a.m.

Practical rules: own at least 3–5 mags per gun, number them with a paint pen, and any mag that stumbles twice gets marked and retired to range duty. Springs wear from cycling, not from sitting loaded.

Red dots: durability & battery honesty

  • Housing alloy matters. 7075 aluminum (the ARO above) survives recoil and drops that crack cheaper 6061 housings. It's the first spec to check on any dot.
  • Battery ratings are quoted at mid brightness. "50,000 hours" means ~5 years always-on at a middle setting — real, but swap in a fresh CR2032 once a year anyway. They cost a dollar; a dead dot costs more.
  • Shake Awake is what makes those numbers usable: the dot sleeps in the safe and wakes the instant you grab the gun, so you never have to remember to turn it on — or off.
  • Footprint before features. Rifle dots want the Aimpoint Micro (T1/T2) pattern; pistol slides want RMR or RMSc/K footprints. The wrong footprint means adapter plates, extra height, and a weaker mount.
  • Enclosed beats open in weather. A tube-style emitter (ARO) shrugs off the rain, dust, and pocket lint that can wash out an exposed open-reflex LED.

Lights, slings & bags — quickly

  • Weapon lights: candela (throw) beats raw lumens for identifying anything past ten yards. And buy the light before the holster — the holster is molded around it.
  • Slings: a quick-adjust two-point is the only style we'd buy in 2026. Padding if the rifle is heavy; skip single-points unless you enjoy being hit by your own rifle.
  • Range bags: rigid frame, lockable zippers on the gun compartment, and labeled pockets beat raw size — and a backpack format keeps both hands free for ammo cans.

Safes: quick-access vs. long-gun

Quick-access boxes (Vaultek, Hornady RAPiD class) are for the one gun you might need at 3 a.m.: biometric or RFID entry that opens in about a second, in the nightstand, bolted down — unbolted, it's just a convenient carrying case for whoever grabs it. This is a kid-and-accident barrier, not burglary protection.

Long-gun safes protect the collection. Buy by real steel (12-gauge door/body or thicker), a genuine fire rating, and weight — and know that most sub-$500 "safes" are technically residential security containers: fine against curious kids and smash-and-grab, not against fire or a pro with time and a pry bar. Buy one size bigger than you think; everyone does eventually.

Why there's no safe card (yet)

We priced Vaultek's line and the Hornady RAPiD hard on Oct 21: pricing is MAP-controlled — the same number at every reputable seller, which means there is no honest deal to card. Same story for Holosun's red dots and most direct-only holster brands (Vedder, CrossBreed sell at fixed prices from their own stores). When a retailer breaks ranks on any of them, it gets a card the same day. Until then, buy the quick-access box that fits your gun and your nightstand, from whoever throws in free shipping.

The 10-second version: ALS holster if the gun is carried openly, a 7075 dot with Shake Awake and a yearly battery swap, OEM-or-Magpul magazines only for serious guns — and never let a $20 no-name magazine be the reason a $600 pistol chokes.

What Actually Matters in a Knife

Knife marketing is steel-name bingo. Here's what separates a knife you'll carry for a decade from one that ends up in a drawer — the same way we'd explain it at the counter.

Heat treat beats steel name

The steel on the box is only half the story — how it was heat-treated matters more. The same D2 can be a chippy 62 HRC or a tough, keen 59 HRC depending on who ran the oven. This is why Civivi punches above its price: WE Knife's heat treats are consistently dialed, so their "budget" D2 and Nitro-V outperform no-name "super steels" from makers cutting corners. A well-treated mid-tier steel at the right hardness beats a poorly treated premium steel every time — and it's easier to sharpen at home.

Lockup is safety

Shake the knife and check for blade play — side-to-side wobble means worn or sloppy tolerances. A good liner lock (both Civivis here) engages early and solidly; a crossbar/AXIS lock (Benchmade) is stronger and ambidextrous; a compression lock (Spyderco) keeps fingers out of the blade path. Whatever the mechanism, it should lock with a positive, repeatable snap and zero vertical rock.

Ergonomics decide what you carry

  • The best knife is the one in your pocket. Under 3 oz disappears; over 4 oz needs a reason. The Baby Banter at 1.99 oz gets carried on days a bigger knife stays home.
  • Handle before blade specs. No hot spots, no sharp scale edges, a grip that works with wet hands. G10 (all the Civivis) is the sweet spot — grippy, tough, cheap.
  • Deployment you can do cold and tired: thumb studs, a flipper, or Spyderco's hole — one-handed, every time, without thinking.
  • Deep-carry clip, tip-up. The knife rides low, comes out oriented right, and doesn't advertise itself.
  • Edge geometry cuts, steel just lasts. A thin, hollow- or flat-ground blade (Elementum) slices cardboard and food better than a thick "hard use" wedge at any steel grade.

The 10-second version: First EDC → Elementum at $30.53, don't overthink it. Small carry → Baby Banter. Hard-use assisted opener, USA-made → the Blur. And when every store charges the same MAP price (Para 3, Bugout), there's no deal — wait for a real one.

The ACID Deep Dive

Partisans will tell you infused cigars aren't "real" cigars. The sales charts — and anyone who's actually smoked a Kuba Kuba — tell a different story. Here's the whole picture.

The Drew Estate story

Jonathan Drew and Marvin Samel started Drew Estate in 1995, selling cigars from a kiosk in New York before relocating to Estelí, Nicaragua — hurricane-battered, dirt cheap, and the heart of cigar country — where exactly two rollers showed up on day one. They partnered with Scott "ACID" Chester, whose industrial-design studio (Arielle Chester Industrial Design — A.C.I.D.) gave the line its name and its graffiti-art look. The motorcycle-rider logo, the color-coded bands, the attitude: all of it was deliberately not your grandfather's cigar brand, and it built the best-selling infused cigar line in America.

Infused ≠ flavored

This is the part the snobs skip. A cheap flavored cigar is sprayed or dipped. ACID tobaccos are cured for months in sealed aroma rooms lined with over 140 essential oils, herbs, and botanicals, so the character is in the leaf itself — Drew Estate guards the exact recipes like trade secrets, because they are. Underneath the aromatics is genuinely good Nicaraguan long filler, which is why a Kuba still tastes like a cigar and not a candle.

The lines worth knowing

  • Kuba Kuba (Blue) — the flagship. Fat 5×54 robusto, Sumatra wrapper, sweet and floral. Start here; it's the best-seller for a reason.
  • Kuba Kuba Maduro — the same blend under dark broadleaf: cocoa and coffee depth, a notch richer.
  • Blondie — 4×38 short smoke in a Connecticut shade wrapper. The 20-minute, coffee-break ACID; also the easiest first cigar to hand a newcomer.
  • Deep Dish — box-pressed 5×58, slow and cool-burning, toasty sweet. The big-ring evening version.
  • Cold Infusion Tea — the lightest of the line, Connecticut-wrapped and refreshing; a morning cigar.
  • Liquid (Red band) — the fuller-bodied end of ACID, no sweetened cap. For smokers who want the infusion turned down and the tobacco turned up.
  • ACID One — the oddball: not infused at all, but cured in red-wine casks. The bridge cigar for traditionalists.

Humidor & storage basics

  • 65–70% humidity, ~70°F. That's the whole target. Two-way Boveda packs are the zero-effort way to hold it — swap them when they go stiff and crunchy.
  • Give ACIDs their own space. Non-negotiable: the aromatics will migrate into traditional cigars stored against them. A separate small humidor or a sealed tupperdor for the infused sticks keeps everybody happy.
  • Rest mail-order sticks 1–2 weeks before smoking. Shipping dries and stresses them; the humidor evens them back out.
  • Keep the cello on in a shared box — it slows aroma transfer and protects wrappers.
  • Gurgling or a tight draw? Dry-box the cigar (unhumidified box, lid cracked) for a day. Over-humidified is the #1 cause of bad burns.

Buying ACIDs smart

Boxes beat singles by a mile. A Kuba Kuba single retails around $11.80; the Atlantic box price above works out to $6.66 a stick — a 44% swing for buying the format you'd buy anyway once you know you like them.

Sampler first, box second. The Seven Wonders exists precisely so you don't commit to 24 of the wrong line. One sampler, one notebook, then the box.

Car Audio: What Actually Matters

Car audio is where spec-sheet lying was perfected. Three numbers cut through all of it.

RMS is real, "peak" is fiction

Every card on this sheet carries a one-line expert rationale, and every power figure we print is RMS — continuous watts the gear handles all day. "Peak" or "max" power is a momentary number measured under conditions you'll never reproduce; a "$40 amp, 2,000 watts!" is making maybe 80 honest watts. Match amp RMS to sub RMS at the same impedance: a 400W RMS sub wants a ~400W RMS mono amp wired to the right ohm load — underpowering a sub and cranking the gain to compensate is how voice coils die (clipping kills, not wattage).

The box is half the sub

A subwoofer in the wrong box is a different, worse subwoofer. Enclosure volume must match the maker's spec (JBL publishes it for the Club 122) — too small strangles low end, too big lets the cone flop and bottom out. Sealed boxes are tight and accurate; ported boxes play louder and lower but must be tuned to the sub. Budget for the right box the way you budget for the sub itself.

Sensitivity is free volume

  • Speakers: sensitivity (dB @ 1W/1m) tells you how loud a speaker gets on the power you actually have. +3 dB is like doubling your amp power — free. Both JBL sets above run 92–93 dB, which is why they work on head-unit power.
  • Impedance is a lever: a 3Ω speaker pulls more watts from the same amp than a 4Ω one. And a switchable-impedance sub (the Club 122's SSI) future-proofs the whole system.
  • Components > coaxials when you can mount the tweeter high; coaxials win on install simplicity. Either beats factory paper cones by a mile.
  • Head units: buy the interface — wireless CarPlay/Android Auto, screen quality, and clean preamp outputs for a future amp. Built-in "50W × 4" is really ~20W RMS × 4 everywhere; plan on an amp if you want real volume.
  • No soundbars. Not in a car, not as a shortcut, not ever. Real speakers in real doors.

The 10-second version: Start with the Sony head unit and the Club 622 doors on factory power — that alone transforms most cars. Then the Club 122 in a spec box on a matched 400W RMS mono amp when you're ready for bass.
